# Character Web

## What It Is
A map of how characters relate to each other and what functions they serve in the story. No character exists in isolation — they exist in relationship to other characters. The web ensures every character earns their place and creates dynamic tension.

## Character Functions

Every character should serve at least one of these functions:

### The Protagonist
- Drives the story through their Want/Need
- The character whose choices matter most
- The audience's emotional entry point

### The Antagonist
- Opposes the protagonist's Want
- Not necessarily a villain — just the force of opposition
- The best antagonists challenge the protagonist's Need too
- **Mirror principle:** The strongest antagonists represent what the protagonist could become (or what they fear becoming)

### The Mentor
- Provides tools, wisdom, or courage — not answers
- Must eventually be removed or surpassed (the hero must stand alone)
- Can be a person, an object, or a memory

### The Ally
- Supports the protagonist's journey
- Often represents a different approach to the same problem
- Tests the protagonist's values through friendship

### The Shapeshifter
- Loyalty or nature is uncertain
- Keeps the audience guessing
- Creates tension in relationships — "can I trust this person?"

### The Trickster
- Provides comic relief and perspective
- Disrupts the status quo
- Often speaks truth through humor

### The Herald
- Announces change
- Delivers the call to adventure or key information
- Can be a person, an event, or a message

## Relationship Dynamics

Between any two characters, define:
1. **What does A want from B?** (desire)
2. **What does B want from A?** (counter-desire)
3. **What's the tension?** (conflicting desires = drama)
4. **How do they change each other?** (impact)

If two characters want the same thing from each other and there's no tension — one of them is redundant.

## The Redundancy Test
For every character, ask: **"What happens to the story if I remove this character?"**
- If the answer is "nothing changes" — cut them or merge them with another character
- Every character must be load-bearing

## Web Mapping (Quick Method)

Draw the protagonist in the center. Around them, place each character with:
- An arrow showing what they want from the protagonist
- An arrow showing what the protagonist wants from them
- A label for the relationship type (ally, rival, mentor, love interest, etc.)
- A note on how the relationship changes by the end

## For Short-Form
In shorts, you typically have 1-3 characters max:
- **Solo:** Protagonist vs. themselves or the environment
- **Duo:** Protagonist + one other (ally, antagonist, or shapeshifter)
- **Trio:** Protagonist + ally + antagonist (the minimal conflict triangle)

The fewer characters, the clearer each relationship must be. Signal it in seconds:
- Body language (close = trust, distant = tension)
- Eye contact (direct = connection, averted = secrets)
- Matching/contrasting visual design

## Examples

**Toy Story character web:**
- Woody (Protagonist/Ruler archetype): Wants to be Andy's favorite
- Buzz (Shapeshifter → Ally): Threatens Woody's position, then becomes his partner
- Andy (Herald/Goal): His attention is what everyone competes for
- Sid (Antagonist): External threat that forces Woody and Buzz to cooperate
- Tension engine: Woody's jealousy of Buzz drives the entire story

**A 30s ad might use:**
- Protagonist: The customer (Everyman archetype)
- Antagonist: The problem
- Mentor: The product
- That's it. Three "characters." Clean web.

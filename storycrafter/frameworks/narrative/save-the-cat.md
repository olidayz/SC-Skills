# Save the Cat

## What It Is
Blake Snyder's beat sheet — the most prescriptive mainstream screenwriting framework. 15 beats mapped to specific page numbers (or time percentages). Designed for commercial filmmaking. Controversial because some say it makes movies formulaic. Useful because it works.

## The 15 Beats

1. **Opening Image** (0-1%) — A snapshot of the world and protagonist BEFORE the journey. Sets tone.
2. **Theme Stated** (5%) — Someone (not the hero) says something that hints at the theme. The hero doesn't get it yet.
3. **Set-Up** (1-10%) — Meet the hero, their world, their flaws. Plant every element you'll need later.
4. **Catalyst** (10%) — The inciting incident. The thing that changes everything. A letter, a death, a discovery, a stranger.
5. **Debate** (10-20%) — Should the hero go? Can they handle it? This is the "last chance to turn back" section.
6. **Break into Two** (20%) — The hero chooses. Enters the new world. ACT 2 begins.
7. **B Story** (22%) — The love story, friendship, or subplot that carries the theme. Often the relationship that teaches the hero what they need to learn.
8. **Fun and Games** (20-50%) — The promise of the premise. Why people bought the ticket. If your movie is "a cop and a dog solve crimes," this is where we see the cop and dog solving crimes.
9. **Midpoint** (50%) — False victory or false defeat. Stakes raise. The fun is over. Clock starts ticking.
10. **Bad Guys Close In** (50-75%) — External pressure increases AND internal flaws resurface. Team fractures. Plan crumbles.
11. **All Is Lost** (75%) — The lowest point. Often includes a "whiff of death" — someone dies, a relationship ends, hope is extinguished.
12. **Dark Night of the Soul** (75-80%) — The hero sits with loss. This is the emotional bottom. The old self must die.
13. **Break into Three** (80%) — A-ha moment. Hero combines what they learned in A and B stories. New plan. New resolve.
14. **Finale** (80-99%) — The hero acts on their new understanding. Confronts the antagonist. Applies their transformation.
15. **Final Image** (99-100%) — Opposite of the opening image. Proves transformation happened.

## When to Use It
- **Best for:** Commercial features, genre films, anything that needs to "work" for a wide audience
- **Tone fit:** All genres — Snyder categorized them (Monster in the House, Golden Fleece, etc.)
- **Format fit:** Features (ideal), 60s ads (heavily compressed — Opening Image → Catalyst → Fun & Games → Final Image), shorts (select 5-6 key beats)
- **Not ideal for:** Arthouse, experimental, non-linear stories

## Snyder's 10 Genres
Useful for finding your story type:
1. Monster in the House (Jaws, Alien)
2. Golden Fleece (quest — Star Wars, Finding Nemo)
3. Out of the Bottle (wish/curse — Big, Groundhog Day)
4. Dude with a Problem (ordinary person, extraordinary situation — Die Hard)
5. Rites of Passage (life transition — Lady Bird)
6. Buddy Love (relationship — Toy Story, When Harry Met Sally)
7. Whydunit (mystery driven by "why" not "who" — Chinatown)
8. The Fool Triumphant (underdog — Forrest Gump, Legally Blonde)
9. Institutionalized (group vs system — One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest)
10. Superhero (extraordinary person — Spider-Man, Gladiator)

## Example
**Legally Blonde:** Opening Image: Elle in her sorority paradise. Catalyst: Warner dumps her. Break into Two: Elle goes to Harvard. Fun & Games: Fish out of water — pink in a sea of gray. Midpoint: Elle wins the internship (false victory). All Is Lost: Callahan hits on her, everyone doubts her. Dark Night: Elle quits. Break into Three: Paulette reminds her who she is. Finale: Elle wins the case using her unique knowledge. Final Image: Elle graduates top of her class — transformed.

## Common Pitfalls
- Following it too rigidly = predictable
- "Fun and Games" ≠ filler. It must escalate.
- Theme Stated beat can feel clunky if too on-the-nose

# Five-Act Structure

## What It Is
Shakespeare's structure — and most classical drama. Expands the 3-act model with more granular rising and falling action. Particularly useful for stories that need a complex middle with multiple reversals.

## The 5 Acts

### Act 1: Exposition
- Introduce the world, characters, and status quo
- End with the inciting incident / hook

### Act 2: Rising Action
- Complications multiply
- Stakes increase
- Protagonist commits deeper
- Alliances and conflicts established

### Act 3: Climax / Turning Point
- The central crisis — the highest point of tension
- A major revelation, confrontation, or irreversible decision
- In Shakespeare, this is often where everything pivots toward tragedy or resolution

### Act 4: Falling Action
- Consequences of the climax unfold
- Characters deal with the fallout
- Final preparations for the resolution
- Often the most emotionally complex act — hope and despair coexist

### Act 5: Denouement / Resolution
- Final confrontation or resolution
- New equilibrium established
- Loose ends tied (or deliberately left open)

## When to Use It
- **Best for:** Complex dramas, tragedies, prestige TV, literary adaptations
- **Tone fit:** Drama, tragedy, political thriller, epic
- **Format fit:** Features with complex plots, limited series, stage adaptations
- **Not ideal for:** Ads, most short-form, simple linear stories

## Example
**Breaking Bad (series arc):** Act 1: Walter White, teacher with cancer. Act 2: Walt enters the drug world, stakes rise, relationships strain. Act 3: "I am the one who knocks" — Walt fully becomes Heisenberg. Act 4: Empire crumbles, Hank discovers the truth, everything falls apart. Act 5: Walt returns, settles all debts, dies.

## vs. Three-Act
- 3-act: simpler, clearer, more commercial
- 5-act: more room for complexity, reversals, moral ambiguity
- Use 5-act when your story needs the middle to breathe and twist

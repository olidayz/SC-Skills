# Theme

## What It Is
The underlying argument your story makes about the human condition. Not the topic (war, love, ambition) — the POSITION on that topic. Theme is what your story believes.

- Topic: "Power"
- Theme: "Power corrupts those who seek it for personal gain"

- Topic: "Love"
- Theme: "Love requires sacrifice of the self we thought we wanted to be"

## Premise vs. Theme

**Premise** = the story situation ("A chef rat in Paris tries to cook at a fine restaurant")
**Theme** = the argument the story makes ("Anyone can create greatness, regardless of origin")

The premise gets people in the door. The theme is why they remember it.

## How Theme Works in Story

Theme is NOT:
- A message you paste on top of the story
- A moral the character states in dialogue
- A lesson the narrator explains

Theme IS:
- Proven through character choices and consequences
- Explored through the gap between Want and Need
- Tested by the antagonist (who represents the counter-argument)
- Stated by the ending (the outcome IS the thematic statement)

## The Thematic Argument

Every theme has two sides:

| Story Element | Thematic Role |
|---------------|---------------|
| Protagonist's journey | Argues FOR the theme |
| Antagonist's worldview | Argues AGAINST the theme |
| Supporting characters | Explore variations/nuances |
| The ending | Settles the argument |

**The Dark Knight:**
- Theme: "Heroism requires incorruptibility"
- Batman (for): Refuses to kill, absorbs blame
- Joker (against): Tries to prove everyone is corruptible
- Harvey Dent (the test): Corruption of the "white knight" proves the Joker partially right
- Ending: Batman takes the blame — heroism costs everything. Theme confirmed through sacrifice.

## Finding Your Theme

1. What does your protagonist NEED to learn? (That's close to your theme.)
2. What does your antagonist believe? (That's the counter-theme.)
3. What does the ending prove? (That IS the theme.)

If you can't articulate your theme — your story might not have one yet. That's okay early on. But by the outline stage, you should know what your story believes.

## Theme in Short-Form

In a 30s ad or short, theme is compressed to a FEELING + a STATEMENT:
- The feeling: what the audience walks away with (hope, awe, urgency)
- The statement: the tagline, caption, or final image that crystallizes meaning
- "Just Do It" is a theme compressed to three words
- A 30s transformation reel's theme might just be: "Change is possible"

## Theme in Ads

Every effective ad has a thematic argument:
- Nike: "Greatness is earned through relentless effort"
- Apple: "Creativity is the highest human expression"
- Dove: "Real beauty exists outside conventional standards"

The product is positioned as the enabler of the thematic promise.

## Common Pitfalls
- Stating the theme in dialogue ("You know what I learned? Family is everything.") — cringe
- No theme at all (story feels hollow, forgettable)
- Contradicting theme with the ending (the story argues one thing but proves another)
- Multiple themes fighting for dominance (pick one primary, let others be undertones)

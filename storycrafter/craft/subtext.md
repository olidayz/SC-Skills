# Subtext

## What It Is
What's really being said underneath what's actually being said. The gap between words and meaning. Characters rarely say what they mean — and when they do, it's either a breakthrough or a breakdown.

## Why It Matters
- Dialogue without subtext is on-the-nose ("I'm angry at you because you betrayed me") — it feels fake
- Dialogue WITH subtext is alive ("Nice shirt. Is that new?") — said to an ex who's clearly moved on
- Subtext respects the audience's intelligence — they get to decode meaning
- In AI filmmaking (often dialogue-light), subtext lives in visuals: what's in the frame that tells you what ISN'T being said?

## How to Write It

### The Rule
**Characters want things from each other but rarely say it directly.**

Ask for every line of dialogue:
1. What does the character LITERALLY say?
2. What do they ACTUALLY mean?
3. What do they WANT from the other person?

If all three answers are the same — the line has no subtext. Rewrite it.

### Techniques

**Deflection:** Character avoids the real topic by talking about something else.
- "How's your mother?" (Real question: "Are we okay?")

**Contradiction:** Character says the opposite of what they feel.
- "I'm fine." (Meaning: I am not fine.)

**Transfer:** Character talks about something else but projects their feelings onto it.
- "This coffee is bitter." (Said during a bitter argument.)

**Silence:** What's NOT said carries the meaning.
- "Do you love me?" / [no response] (The silence IS the answer.)

**Action vs. Words:** Character says one thing, does another.
- "I don't care" [while carefully arranging their ex's things in a box]

## Visual Subtext (for filmmaking)
When you can't use dialogue, subtext lives in:
- **Object placement:** A wedding ring removed and placed on a table
- **Spatial relationship:** Two people in the same room but on opposite sides
- **Eye direction:** Looking away during an important moment
- **Color:** Warm tones cooling as a relationship deteriorates
- **Repetition:** Showing the same location in different emotional states

## For Short-Form
You have seconds, not pages. Subtext in shorts is almost entirely VISUAL:
- A look between two people that says everything
- An object that represents what can't be spoken
- A gesture that contradicts a caption/text overlay
- The gap between upbeat music and sad visuals

## Example
**Before Sunrise — the whole movie is subtext:**
Every conversation about philosophy, art, and life is actually about: "I'm falling in love with you and I'm terrified because we only have one night."
Neither character says "I love you" until it's too late.

## Common Pitfalls
- Over-explaining subtext to the audience (trust them)
- No subtext at all (characters become mouthpieces for the writer)
- Subtext so deep nobody gets it (there should be two clear layers, not seven)
